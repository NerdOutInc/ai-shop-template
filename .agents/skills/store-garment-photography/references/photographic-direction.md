# Direct a photograph rather than an isolated model

Reference photographs are useful for decisions about attention, light, framing and gesture. Inspect the actual references and describe the parts to borrow. A retailer's name alone is not photographic direction. The identity reference supplies the person; the garment reference supplies clothing; a style reference supplies photographic decisions. Do not accidentally copy one reference's model, pose and light into every output.

## A moment with physical support

Choose a small action or pause that explains the pose: someone listening beside a window, resting on grass, leaning into a wall or sitting at the edge of a step. These are examples, not mandatory scenes. Bodies have weight. Hands pressing on a surface, hips on a seat and bent limbs should produce plausible contact and fabric compression.

An interesting everyday photograph need not have a prop or a conspicuous activity. If removing an unwanted prop makes the hands or expression nonsensical, replace the scene/gesture rather than substituting a harmless object into the old grip. Let the caller's brief determine exclusions and settings.

## One shared lighting situation

Before generation, identify the source/direction of light, which objects block it, contact points and the areas that must remain darker. The person and surroundings share exposure and shadow logic.

In directional light, a brow can shade the eyes, the nose/chin can cast shadows and sleeves can shade arms. Contact with a wall, seat or ground creates darkness. Let part of the design enter shade. A person with a bright edge and evenly filled face may look pasted into a scene when no light source explains it. Natural edge highlights are possible; the failure is an unexplained private softbox or rim light for the model.

Shade and window light need coherent shadows too. Do not force hard sunlight onto every scene, darken everything with a generic overlay or recover every shadow as though it were a studio catalog shot. Preserve scene lighting when compositing the ink.

## Camera and focus belong to the moment

Give the camera a plausible nearby position and ordinary focal perspective. Slightly uneven framing, a partial crop and an off-camera response can be natural when the activity explains them. Avoid turning those into compulsory quirks.

The face, shirt and nearby environment should have compatible focus. A razor-sharp model against uniformly smeared scenery often feels detached; readable nearby objects help connect them. Depth of field can still be shallow when distance and lens behavior explain it. Do not make grain, blur, an orange grade or exaggerated pores do the work of believable photography.

Complicated equipment and fabricated rock surfaces are frequent failure points. Use inspected location references when a specific setting matters. A close, simple background can be more convincing than a broad invented environment. Inspect geometry and contact rather than accepting a scene because the prompt named the right place.

## Studio and lifestyle have different emphasis

Studio: a simple setting, usable view of the garment, natural expression and believable weight shift. Frame close enough for the requested print inspection; avoid automatically stepping back to a full-body fashion shot.

Lifestyle: attention belongs to the moment. Some front artwork may be occluded or in shadow. Do not move hands out of the way merely to make a catalog display, unless the user's requested shot requires it. Preserve measured print placement and let real foreground occlusion hide the ink.

Across a set, vary the exposure rather than applying shirt swaps. Keep identity and garment continuity while varying pose, expression, framing and light in ways the scene supports. Review the whole set for repetitive body language and artificial lighting, not just each photograph in isolation.

## A practical prompt structure

State reference roles, exact garment construction/color, specific moment, body support, camera/framing, source/direction of light, expression and relevant exclusions. Keep the shirt blank for precise later compositing. Supply enough concrete decisions to guide the image, then judge the result: a well-written prompt cannot certify an impossible hand or an invented hem.
